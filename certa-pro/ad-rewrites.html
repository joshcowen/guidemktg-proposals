Ad Rewrites

Here's what we changed in your current ads and why. Every edit comes back to one shift: moving from positioning CertaPro as the hero to positioning your customer as the hero — with CertaPro as the guide who helps them win.

The Core Idea

Your customer is the hero. You're the guide.

Most companies market themselves the same way: lead with credentials, list services, and ask for the sale. And most customers tune it out, because nothing in that message is about them.

The StoryBrand framework flips this. Your customer is walking around with a problem they want solved. They're not looking for a company that's been around 14 years — they're looking for someone who understands their frustration and can make it go away. When your message leads with their pain first, they stop scrolling.

These rewrites don't change who CertaPro is. They change who the story is about.

What changed and why

HEADLINE
From credential to pain point
"Grand Haven's Most Trusted Painters" is a claim about CertaPro. The reader hears it and thinks "sure, everyone says that." The rewrite opens with the fear the customer is already carrying — is this person going to show up? That's the real question in every homeowner's head before they hire a painter. When your headline names that fear, they stop scrolling because you're talking about them, not about you. The character has a problem. Lead with their problem, not your resume.
BODY OPENING
From CertaPro's story to their story
The original opens with "CertaPro Painters has a 4.7-star rating for a reason." That sentence makes CertaPro the subject. The rewrite opens with "Most Grand Haven homeowners have at least one contractor story they'd rather forget." Now the customer is the subject. You're showing empathy before you pitch anything — which is the guide move. The customer needs to feel understood before they'll listen to your solution. A guide demonstrates empathy before authority. Empathy first, credentials second.
SERVICES LIST
Kept the list, changed the frame
"Interior, exterior, cabinets, decks, we do it all" is hero language — a capability list, not a customer benefit. But the list itself is worth keeping: a lot of homeowners don't know they can hire the same company for their deck that they hire for their interior. That specificity pre-qualifies the right customer. The rewrite keeps the list but wraps it in a promise of experience: "here's what you get" — not "here's our menu." Same services, different story. The guide describes the plan — what the customer's experience will be — not just their capabilities.
SOCIAL PROOF
Moved to the end, reframed
The original leads with the star rating as proof CertaPro is good. The rewrite saves social proof for the end and reframes it around the customer outcome: "4.7 stars from over 40 Grand Haven homeowners who finally got the experience they deserved." Same 4.7 stars — but now it's proof that other people like them solved the exact problem you just described. Authority comes after empathy. Proof closes the loop — it doesn't open the pitch.

What changed and why

HOOK
From fear to a standard
The original hook opens with three anxiety questions. The rewrite opens with a statement of what the homeowner deserves: "You deserve a painter who respects your time and your home." That's a different kind of pattern interrupt. Instead of activating worry, it sets a standard — and then the rest of the ad shows CertaPro is the company that meets it. It also names the internal and philosophical problem in one line: a contractor who doesn't respect your time is disrespecting you, not just running late. The customer's deepest problem isn't external (late painter) — it's internal (feeling disrespected) and philosophical (you deserve better). Lead with what they deserve, not just what they fear.
SCENE 1
Simpler, cleaner problem statement
The original Scene 1 pivots to CertaPro too fast. The rewrite stays in the problem for one clean sentence: late crews, weak communication, touch-up work that should've been right the first time. Three specific problems. No editorializing. No "you're being smart" kicker — just a plain statement of what's been happening. Specific is more believable than emphatic. Name the external problem plainly and specifically before you introduce the guide. The customer needs to feel seen, not coached on how to feel.
SCENE 2
Services list back in, framed as customer benefit
The earlier draft cut "interior, exterior, cabinets, decks" entirely. That was a mistake. A homeowner who needs their deck painted may not assume the same company does that — this line does real qualifying work. The rewrite puts it back but wraps it in the customer's outcome: "you get a finish that looks clean and holds up." Same services, different story. CertaPro is still the guide delivering a result — not a company listing what it sells. Pre-qualifying language helps the right customer self-select. Keep specifics that do that job, just frame them around what the customer gets, not what you offer.
CTA
Direct ask, no setup needed
By the time the viewer reaches the CTA, they've heard what they deserve and what CertaPro delivers. The CTA doesn't need to close with another emotional statement — it just needs to tell them what to do. "Schedule your free estimate with CertaPro Painters of Grand Haven" is specific, confident, and removes any ambiguity about who this is for or what happens next. A strong CTA names the action and the company. The emotional work is already done — just give them the clear next step.
